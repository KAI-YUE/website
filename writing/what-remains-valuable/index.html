When Building Gets Cheaper, What Is Still Worth Building?

September 11, 2026 in Building Businesses

I used to ask whether AI could help me build a product. Working on an investment-education community, merchant automation, and a game-creation platform has made another question harder to ignore: if the same tools become available to everyone, why would someone choose what I build?

Faster implementation helps me test an idea. It does not tell me whether the idea deserves a customer’s time or money.

An earlier version of this essay proposed a principle: build a business that could not exist without AI. A friend asked whether that was simply another way of saying “AI-native.” Fair criticism. Dependence on AI explains how a product becomes possible; it does not establish demand, a viable business model, or a reason to choose it over alternatives.

I still care about the philosophy behind a business: whose interests it serves and which tradeoffs I am willing to accept. But I need to separate those commitments from claims about what will succeed. The following are working tests drawn from my projects, not laws established by their success.

1. Reassess What Customers Are Paying For

I built an investment-education community through YouTube and a paid Telegram group. I shared investment perspectives and taught people to use Python to explore investment questions. The private membership cost $30 per month.

As AI tutoring improved, I began questioning how much value another explanation or article would add. I reduced my activity while reassessing the offering. That was my judgment about a changing alternative, not a measured finding that AI had replaced the service or that members no longer valued it.

The experience changed the question I bring to a product:

If the customer can get the explanation or output elsewhere cheaply, what are they still paying us to accomplish?

Possible answers include completing a task, helping someone follow through, or providing a community they want to participate in. Each needs evidence. I cannot assume that people who paid for one service will pay for its proposed replacement.

The same test applies to merchant automation. A stock alert has limited value if the owner still has to investigate everything manually. I need to understand what happens after the alert, where the owner loses time, and which decisions they want help with. Different merchants may need different answers; if each answer requires extensive custom engineering, that also changes the economics of the business.

2. Follow Creation Through to Its Outcome

I am building a platform for game creation and publishing, and using it to develop my own game. That gives me direct feedback on the creation workflow. It does not yet tell me whether other creators will finish games, attract players, or earn money through it.

This is where my personal ambition meets a difficult business question. I want more people to express themselves, find an audience, and earn from their work. Easier creation could help. It could also increase the supply of games without increasing the attention available to each one.

After making creation easier, which obstacle have we left the creator to solve alone?

For this project, I want to examine completion, publishing, discovery, and whether players return. A generated prototype is evidence of technical capability. A creator returning to finish a project, or a player choosing to come back, would answer a different and more useful question about the product.

Helping people retain agency and participate in the value they create is a design commitment I choose. It is not a prediction of commercial success. I would judge the platform poorly if it encouraged people to spend more on generation while leaving them no closer to the outcome they came for.

3. Treat Defensibility as Something to Demonstrate

Better models can help my product and a competitor’s product at the same time. Calling the architecture agentic does not explain why a customer would stay.

In merchant software, understanding a workflow may help me win an initial customer. It becomes a stronger advantage only if that understanding leads to a service customers rely on and improvements I can reuse across deployments. Bespoke work that starts from scratch each time may support a services business, but it does not automatically create a scalable software advantage.

In a creator platform, relationships between creators and audiences could become valuable. I should not call that a network effect merely because both groups have accounts. I would need evidence that their participation improves the experience for others: better discovery, useful feedback, or repeat support that would be difficult to recreate elsewhere.

If another team uses the same models and reproduces the visible features, what would customers still miss?

That answer may be dependable execution, accumulated workflow knowledge, or relationships people value. These are candidates to test. I cannot claim them as a moat before customers behave as though they matter.

Test the Principles: Dotcom Déjà Vu

A convincing principle is easy to invent after a company succeeds. We remember the winners, overlook the failures, and mistake a retrospective explanation for foresight.

I’m exploring that problem through Dotcom Déjà Vu: what might the next Google look like before its success becomes obvious?

Any algorithm addressing that question must choose what to reward. Growth? Technical advantage? Commercial durability? Social usefulness? Those judgments shape its results before a reader sees a single company. Social usefulness and investor returns may also lead to different rankings; I should make that choice explicit. The aim is to surface candidates for investigation and make the reasoning open to scrutiny.

Historical comparisons provide a test:

What was knowable then?

Could we compare Google with AltaVista in 1998 without knowing the outcome? Or Facebook with Friendster at their historical decision points?

The constraint is no hindsight. Later outcomes can test a principle, but they cannot supply evidence that was unavailable at the time. Choosing only famous winners and their failed rivals would still bias the exercise. A stronger test would fix the criteria and candidate pool before looking at outcomes, include companies that disappeared from view, and compare against a simple baseline. A model’s familiarity with historical outcomes is another source of leakage to address. Even a successful historical test would not establish that an algorithm can predict the next winner.

These are requirements for a credible test, not a claim that I have already satisfied them. Building the product gives me a way to examine where my judgments hold and where they fail.

What Remains Valuable?

I do not know which abilities will remain scarce as models improve. For now, the work I need to do is concrete: understand what customers are trying to accomplish, test whether the product helps, and change course when the evidence disagrees with my story.

Philosophy helps me decide which outcomes I want to pursue and which costs I refuse to pass on to other people. Customer behavior tells me whether the offering is useful enough to choose. Neither lets me skip the other.
