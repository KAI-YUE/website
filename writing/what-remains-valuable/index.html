Math May Be Solved. Coding May Be Solved. But Not Philosophy.

September 11, 2026 in Building Businesses

A few years ago, I worried that AI might solve coding.

Now I think that question was too small.

Suppose AI could solve most mathematical and programming problems we put before it. Work that once required years of technical training could become available on demand.

We would still have to decide which problems deserve attention.

What should we build? Whose interests should it serve? What consequences are we willing to accept?

Better tools can inform those decisions. They cannot relieve us of responsibility for them.

In my work, this becomes business philosophy: deciding what to build, whose interests it serves, and which tradeoffs we accept. Those decisions shape the product, its architecture, and its business model.

My background is in machine learning research. Today, I design and implement agentic systems and build products around them. Technical execution still matters, but faster implementation brings product decisions into focus sooner: who needs this, what should it do, and why should it exist?

I’m exploring these questions through Dotcom Déjà Vu, a product built around a deceptively simple question: what might the next Google look like before its success becomes obvious?

Any algorithm I build to answer that question must choose what to reward. Growth? Technical advantage? Commercial durability? Social usefulness? Those choices shape the results before a reader sees a single company.

The aim is to surface candidates for investigation and make the reasoning open to scrutiny. Historical comparisons offer a way to test that reasoning against what was knowable at the time. They do not establish that an algorithm can predict the next winner.

The following three principles are hypotheses I bring to that work.

1. Depend on the New Infrastructure

Every technological shift creates the temptation to take an old product and attach the new technology to it.

The Internet had this.

Mobile had this.

Cloud had this.

Now AI has it.

So I have started using a simple test:

Remove the new infrastructure. Does the product remain fundamentally the same product?

If the answer is yes, the technology may be improving the product without really changing what the product is.

That can still be useful. But it is not the part of a technological shift that interests me most.

The more interesting question is:

What becomes possible now that was previously unreasonable, uneconomical, or impossible?

Google was not simply a printed directory placed on the Internet.

The Internet made it possible to continuously organize and navigate a web that was itself growing at Internet scale.

The smartphone similarly did more than shrink websites onto smaller screens. A device with a camera, location, connectivity, identity, and constant presence created entirely new product behaviors.

I think AI deserves the same treatment.

Instead of asking:

Where can I add AI?

I increasingly want to ask:

What no longer needs to exist in its old form because AI exists?

There is a difference between:

[ \text{Old Product} + \text{New Technology} ]

and:

[ \text{New Technology} \rightarrow \text{New Product Form} ]

The second is where I want to look.

2. Make the Goodness of AI Something People Can Feel

A strange thing has happened around AI.

The technology is advancing extraordinarily quickly, yet much of the public experience of AI is framed through anxiety.

Jobs disappearing.

Models replacing people.

Benchmarks climbing.

Another profession being automated.

Another prediction that everything is about to change.

These questions matter. But they are not the only way people should encounter a new technology.

For me, human value starts with a simple question:

Where does AI actually touch a human life?

Not where a benchmark improves.

Not where a model sounds impressive.

Where does someone actually feel:

I couldn’t do this before. Now I can.

That might be a small business owner gaining capabilities that previously required a specialized team.

It might be someone who cannot program turning an idea into a working game.

It might be a person learning, creating, communicating, or exploring beyond what used to be their practical limits.

This is one reason I care about products that interact directly with human needs, behavior, creativity, relationships, and the physical world.

Technology becomes much more interesting when its value escapes the benchmark and enters ordinary experience.

The best argument for AI may not be an argument at all.

It may be an experience.

I don’t want AI to feel valuable because somebody tells people that the model is intelligent.

I want people to encounter something they could not reasonably do before and feel the difference themselves.

That, to me, is a much more optimistic way to build.

3. Build Something the Next Model Makes Stronger

Product durability raises an uncomfortable question every AI product should face.

What happens when the next model arrives?

Suppose the next generation of GPT, Claude, Gemini, or another foundation model becomes dramatically better.

Does my product become better with it?

Or does the model simply absorb the reason my product existed?

That distinction matters.

A fragile AI product can look like this:

[ \text{Product Value}

\text{Something the current model cannot quite do yet} ]

That creates a dangerous relationship with the underlying infrastructure.

Every improvement to the infrastructure becomes a threat.

The better the model gets, the smaller the product becomes.

I would rather build the opposite kind of system:

[ \text{Better Infrastructure} \rightarrow \text{Better Product} ]

The model can improve.

The cost of intelligence can fall.

Context windows can grow.

Agents can become more capable.

And the product becomes more useful because of it.

That means the durable part of the product has to live somewhere above raw model capability.

It might live in workflow.

It might live in distribution, community, accumulated user context, proprietary interaction data, network effects, or deep integration with a real-world process.

The exact moat will differ.

The test is simpler:

When the next model arrives, does it upgrade my product — or replace it?

I find that question much more useful than asking whether an AI startup has a “moat” in the abstract.

From Capability to Judgment

These three ideas connect more than I initially realized.

The first asks:

What became newly possible?

The second asks:

Who actually becomes better off because of it?

The third asks:

What remains when the technology gets even better?

Or, more compactly:

[ \text{Possibility} \rightarrow \text{People} \rightarrow \text{Durability} ]

This is where my answer to the original question has changed.

When answers become cheap, I don’t think human value disappears.

The scarce part moves.

Building becomes easier.

Choosing what deserves to be built does not.

But Good Principles Are Cheap Too

There is a problem with everything I just wrote.

It sounds convincing.

That does not make it true.

It is remarkably easy to study a successful company after the fact and construct a beautiful explanation for why it succeeded.

We remember Google.

We remember Facebook.

We remember the companies that survived long enough to become examples in books, classes, and founder essays.

The failures become footnotes.

Then we look backward and discover principles that seem obvious only because we already know the ending.

I don’t want to do that.

This is the historical test behind Dotcom Déjà Vu.

The question behind it is simple:

What was knowable then?

If today were 1998, what could we actually have known about Google?

Could we have compared it with AltaVista without knowing what happened next?

Could we look at Facebook and Friendster at their historical decision points without allowing the next twenty years to leak backward into our judgment?

The constraint is deliberately uncomfortable:

No hindsight.

Future outcomes can be used to test a principle.

They cannot be used to construct the historical observation.

The principles in this essay need to earn their place in the product through those tests.

I want to know whether they survive contact with history.

Maybe some will.

Maybe some will disappear.

Maybe the things that look profound in 2026 would have been completely useless in 1998.

That possibility is exactly what makes the experiment interesting.

Different technology. Familiar feeling.

What Remains Valuable?

I started with a question:

What remains valuable when answers become cheap?

My current answer is judgment — but not judgment in the abstract.

It is the ability to recognize what a new infrastructure genuinely makes possible.

To turn that possibility into something people can actually feel.

And to build something that becomes stronger, rather than obsolete, when the infrastructure improves again.

AI can make answers cheaper.

It can make implementation cheaper.

It may eventually make many forms of intelligence extraordinarily cheap.

But that only makes another question more important:

What should we do with it?

I don’t know whether my current principles are right.

Building the product gives me a way to test them. Choosing its goals, examining its assumptions, and taking responsibility for its consequences remain part of the work.
