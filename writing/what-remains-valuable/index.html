Math May Be Solved. Coding May Be Solved. But Not Philosophy.

September 11, 2026 in Building Businesses

A few years ago, I worried that AI might solve coding.

Now I think that question was too small.

Suppose AI could solve most mathematical and programming problems we put before it. Work that once required years of technical training could become available on demand.

We would still have to decide which problems deserve attention.

In my work, this becomes business philosophy: deciding what to build, whose interests it serves, and which tradeoffs we accept. Those decisions shape the product, its architecture, and its business model. Better tools can inform them; responsibility remains with us.

My background is in machine learning research. Today, I design and implement agentic systems and build products around them. Faster implementation brings the harder product questions into focus sooner. These are three principles I use to examine them.

1. Build Businesses That Depend on AI

My test is simple:

Remove AI. Is the business still viable?

I’m interested in businesses that become viable because AI exists. Their core offering depends on capabilities that would otherwise be too costly or impractical to provide. Remove AI, and the business no longer works.

But capability alone gives people little reason to care. The business must turn it into something people can experience: freedom to create, access to help, or relief from work that previously consumed their time.

AI makes the business possible. The business makes AI’s human value tangible.

That is the relationship I want to build around: a business born from AI, earning its place by improving people’s lives.

2. Help People Create, Earn, and Feel Valued

Human value goes beyond completing tasks faster. I want to build businesses that give people a way to express themselves, find an audience, and earn from work that matters to them.

Platforms such as YouTube and Steam illustrate the ambition: a place where creative work can reach people who appreciate it and support its creator. The value includes recognition, independence, and the possibility of building a livelihood.

AI can lower the barriers to making something. The business must also consider what happens afterward: how creators reach an audience, retain agency over their work, and participate in the value it generates. More output alone does not fulfill that promise.

Do people feel that their ideas and contributions matter—and have a real opportunity to flourish?

That is the standard I want to build toward. Efficiency can support it, but the purpose is to expand what people can contribute and what they can receive in return.

3. Build a Moat That Strengthens with AI

Better models can improve a product while giving competitors the same advantage. A business moat requires something harder to reproduce: value that accumulates through use and gives people stronger reasons to return.

For a creator platform, that could be the relationships between creators and audiences, a trusted reputation, or discovery that improves through feedback. These become defensible when they help people find work they value and help creators reach people who will support them. A growing content library alone does not establish a moat.

AI should improve the offering; participation should strengthen the business.

The ambition is a reinforcing cycle: better tools help creators produce worthwhile work; that work attracts audiences; audience feedback and support give creators reasons to keep building. The moat depends on whether those connections become more valuable as the platform grows.

If competitors have access to the same models, why would creators and audiences still choose us?

That is the test I want to apply as AI advances: whether the business earns lasting preference through the value people build together.

Together, the three principles form a sequence:

AI makes it possible → People find value → Participation builds a moat

Why can this business exist now? How does it help people flourish? What becomes harder to reproduce as it grows?

Test the Principles: Dotcom Déjà Vu

A convincing principle is easy to invent after a company succeeds. We remember the winners, overlook the failures, and mistake a retrospective explanation for foresight.

I’m exploring that problem through Dotcom Déjà Vu: what might the next Google look like before its success becomes obvious?

Any algorithm addressing that question must choose what to reward. Growth? Technical advantage? Commercial durability? Social usefulness? Those judgments shape its results before a reader sees a single company. The aim is to surface candidates for investigation and make the reasoning open to scrutiny.

Historical comparisons provide a test:

What was knowable then?

Could we compare Google with AltaVista in 1998 without knowing the outcome? Or Facebook with Friendster at their historical decision points?

The constraint is no hindsight. Later outcomes can test a principle, but they cannot supply evidence that was unavailable at the time. Even a successful historical test would not establish that an algorithm can predict the next winner.

These principles are hypotheses. Building the product gives me a way to examine where they hold and where they fail.

What Remains Valuable?

My answer is judgment: choosing worthwhile goals, testing assumptions, and taking responsibility for the consequences.

AI can help with all of that. Philosophy is not a claim that machines cannot reason about values. It is the work of deciding which values our products should serve—and accepting that technical capability alone cannot settle the choice.
